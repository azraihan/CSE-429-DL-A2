Finally NFKC-normalise and collapse whitespace.

REFLECTION — WHAT I ACCEPTED, CHANGED, AND REJECTED
================================================================================

WHAT I REJECTED

The biggest thing I rejected was the AI's Kaggle setup, twice. Its first design used
`pip download` without `--no-deps`, which pulled 2.7 GB of wheels including torch;
installing that offline could have replaced Kaggle's CUDA build and silently cost me
the GPU. Its second design saved the staged assets as thousands of loose files, and
Kaggle kept only the rendered notebook and dropped 4.6 GB of data. Both wasted a run.
After the second failure I stopped accepting "this should work" and told it I did not
have time for more trial and error. That is when it started actually executing the
failure paths instead of reasoning about them, and it immediately found two more real
bugs: a `subprocess(stdout=sys.stdout)` call that raises inside Jupyter, and an
over-strict assertion that would have failed after a successful two-hour build.

The lesson I take from that: an AI's confidence is not evidence. Asking "are you sure"
is worthless; asking "prove it by running it" is not.

WHAT I CHANGED

I reviewed the held-out ground truth by eye instead of trusting it, and found the
mathematical notation was coming out as Korean characters and that table structure was
being lost. The AI had built that oracle from the PDF text layer and reported OCR
scores against it. When I pushed back, it traced the cause to a real bug - the PDF's
ToUnicode values are truncated to 16 bits, dropping every math italic into the Hangul
block - and repaired it. Overall OCR token-F1 went from 0.785 to 0.866, and three maths
pages that looked like model failures turned out to be oracle failures. If I had
accepted the first number, our Section 5 evidence would have been measured against
broken ground truth.

I also questioned why the ground truth came from transcription at all rather than being
hand-typed, which is what the course workbook asks for. The AI's answer was reasonable
(born-digital PDFs make the text layer authoritative for prose) but it admitted it had
deviated without telling me. That deviation is now documented in the form rather than
hidden.

WHAT I ACCEPTED

I accepted the engineering judgement where it was backed by measurement. The clearest
case was the layout stage: the AI argued that morphology cannot separate our two
columns because the gutter is narrower than the kernel needed to join words into lines,
and it demonstrated this by showing the columns welded together before fixing it with a
banded projection profile. I could check that reasoning against the rendered overlays,
so I took it.

I also accepted its correction of our own A1 form. It measured the corpus and found that
essentially every quantitative claim in A1 Section 3 was wrong - we had reported 51.8k
pages and ~150 words per page against an actual 1,823 pages at 432 words, and a 61% cs
skew that does not exist. That was uncomfortable but correct, and the corrected numbers
made our data-speciality argument stronger, not weaker: evidence inside figures and
tables turned out to be 56% of questions rather than the 21% we guessed.

WHAT I WOULD DO DIFFERENTLY

I would verify the environment before building for it. Most of the lost time came from
the AI validating code on my Windows machine and assuming Kaggle would match, when the
transformers version, the notebook stdout, and Kaggle's output persistence all differed.
I would also have caught the ground-truth problem sooner if I had reviewed the oracle
when it was first generated rather than after the scores were reported.

REFLECTION — WHAT I ACCEPTED, CHANGED, AND REJECTED

WHAT I REJECTED

I rejected the first S3 evaluation number outright. 0.31 against 0.75+ on the
other two strata was too large a gap to write down and move on from, so instead of
accepting "the system is just bad at tables" I asked to see the actual wrong
answers before believing the score. That's what surfaced the normalization
argument-order bug — the scorer was comparing a normalized gold string against an
un-normalized prediction, which fails on formatting alone, not on correctness. If
I had taken the first number, our own Section 5 would have understated the system
by roughly double, and Section 7's failure analysis would have been about a bug in
our scorer instead of the model.

I also rejected "citations work" as a claim I hadn't tried to break. Asking the AI
to check whether cited spans actually matched what the model used — rather than
accepting that the pipeline produced a citation at all — found a real 3-in-30
mismatch rate that a plausible-looking system would otherwise have shipped with.

I rejected the first draft of the Section 6 write-up on the word "understands."
It's a small word, but it claimed something the failure taxonomy directly
contradicts — a system that composes a ratio from two table cells wrong doesn't
understand the table, it's matching spans. I'd rather defend an accurate sentence
than an impressive one.

WHAT I CHANGED

I changed how abstentions are counted in Section 1's NFR. The written rule was
already correct — abstain does not equal wrong — but the worked example scored it
as zero anyway, silently rewarding a system for never admitting uncertainty. That
would have undermined the entire point of a robustness NFR. I fixed the example
to match the rule we'd already agreed on, rather than accepting the version that
happened to be in the document.

I also changed the re-search budget after seeing the actual failure it caused —
an unbounded re-search loop that ran 40+ times on one adversarial question. The
fix (cap at one attempt, then abstain) is defensible on its own, but I only
trusted it because I saw the trace log that produced the hang, not because the AI
told me it would work.

The biggest change was the abstention threshold itself. It started as a number
picked by feel (0.6, on a raw uncalibrated confidence score), and I only found out
it was wrong because the failure taxonomy flagged over-cautious abstention as a
real category, which led to a reliability diagram, which showed the raw score was
badly overconfident at the top end. Recalibrating it moved the final pooled F1
from 0.732 to 0.741 and nearly halved S3's abstention count without adding wrong
answers. None of that would have happened if I'd stopped at the first "final"
number.

WHAT I ACCEPTED

I accepted the derivation for the 0.70 headline target once it was grounded in an
actual floor-baseline run (0.41) rather than asserted. A round number with no
anchor isn't something I'd have signed off on; a round number that sits between a
measured floor and a plausible ceiling for comparable systems is.

I accepted the cross-encoder rerank choice because it came with a benchmark
against the alternative, not just a name. 74% versus 61% top-1 accuracy for 180ms
